AI Slop Is Flooding
Health Content Online.

Older Americans are the most exposed — and the least equipped to tell what's real.
By Gordon Cheng · Inclusive Design, SVA Spring 2026
AI-generated health misinformation is flooding social media at an unprecedented rate. Deepfake doctors, fabricated clinical studies, and synthetic testimonials are being used to sell supplements to people who can't tell the difference. The most vulnerable group — Americans over 65 — is also the one with the fewest protections. No platform has age-specific safeguards. The regulatory framework was built for a world of human-authored content. And the trend across every major platform is toward less moderation, not more.

Editorial

Feedback from last week

Two Things to Act On

Narrow the scope.

AI slop is broad. Focus on something specific and traceable: health supplement advertising. Fake doctor videos, bogus before-and-after photos, AI-generated testimonials — all pushing pills.

Test the broken loop.

The ecosystem map assumes the trust loop — family, doctors — is broken. But is it? Go talk to actual families. Find out if children really can't see what their parents consume online, and whether they even try to intervene.

So I designed an interview guide and talked to someone.

Interview #01

Interviewee

Fiona, UX Designer

Seattle-based. Parents both 58, living in Beijing.
22 min · Remote · April 2, 2026
Fiona's father does freelance cultural work. Her mother isn't working. Both have a lot of free time, but the family doesn't really talk about health with each other. Fiona's words: "We live fairly separate lives." She's a UX designer, but when it comes to her parents' online behavior, she admits she doesn't know much. What they watch, what they click, what they believe — she mostly can't see any of it.
Father

Only uses WeChat + Douyin. Can't figure out new apps. Orders food through WeChat mini-programs because that's all he knows.

Forwards conspiracy theories and classical poetry. Into Buddhism/Daoism. Won't exercise, won't get check-ups.

"The body ages naturally."
Mother

Uses WeChat, Toutiao, Xiaohongshu, Alipay, and games. Refuses short video apps but reads Toutiao news daily — mixed quality.

Buys vitamins and calcium supplements. Where'd she hear about them? Fiona doesn't know. Thinks US products are better.

Interview Guide

What I Asked

Background

1. Tell me about your parents — age, where they live, working or retired?
2. What devices and apps do they use? How comfortable are they with tech?

Health Info Behavior

3. When they have a health concern, where do they look first?
4. Have you seen them watching health videos or reading health articles online?

Family Dynamics

8. When you catch them believing something off, what do you do? How do they react?
9. Do you feel responsible for filtering what they see? What makes it hard?

Misleading Content

5. Have they ever shared a health tip that turned out to be wrong?
6. Have they bought supplements they found through social media or ads?
7. Could they tell the difference between a real doctor and an AI-generated one?

Design Prompt

10. If you could build one tool to protect your parents from bad health content online, what would it do?

These 10 questions were designed around a 20-minute interview. Questions 5–7 are the core — everything else builds context or opens design space.

Findings

What Came Up

Fiona can't see where her mom gets health info

Her mother buys supplements, but Fiona has no idea which app or article led to it.

"It's definitely media-influenced, but I don't know where she saw it."

The parents are vulnerable in opposite ways

Father sees very little online, but can't tell what's real. Mother sees a lot, but the sources are all over the place.

Not a typical family for this study

They don't forward health tips. Don't buy miracle cures. Fiona actually has to drag them to the doctor.

"My parents are outliers. They don't even do health check-ups."

But the father would probably fall for AI content

He shares conspiracy posts. He's behind on all tech. His friend group passes around AI-generated videos already.

"His persona is the type who would believe AI content."

Surprise from the interview

Why AI Slop Is a Bigger Deal
in the U.S. Than in China

United States

Labor is expensive.
Fake content used to be costly to produce at scale.
AI cut that cost to near zero.

AI is a new problem here.

China

Labor is cheap.
Content farms were already everywhere, run by people.
AI actually costs more than hiring someone.

AI doesn't change much.

AI slop hits hardest in places where fake content was too expensive to mass-produce before. That's the U.S. Our research focus makes sense — but it's not a universal story.
