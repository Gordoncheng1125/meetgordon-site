AI Slop Is Flooding
Health Content Online.

Older Americans are the most exposed — and the least equipped to tell what's real.
By Gordon Cheng · Inclusive Design, SVA Spring 2026
AI-generated health misinformation is flooding social media faster than platforms can moderate it. Deepfake doctors, fabricated clinical studies, and synthetic testimonials are being used to sell supplements to people who can't tell the difference. The most vulnerable group — Americans over 65 — is also the one with the fewest protections. No platform has age-specific safeguards. The regulatory framework was built for a world of human-authored content. And the trend across every major platform is toward less moderation, not more.

Narrow the scope.

AI slop is broad. Focus on something specific and traceable: health supplement advertising. Fake doctor videos, bogus before-and-after photos, AI-generated testimonials — all pushing pills.

THE SYSTEMIC INSIGHT

The Platform Paradox

AI-generated content costs almost nothing to produce. More content means more ad slots, more revenue. The thing that’s eroding trust in health information is also making platforms money. They have no financial reason to stop it, and their legal teams make sure they don’t have to.

The system isn’t broken — it’s doing what it was built to do: optimize for profit, not safety.

DECONSTRUCTING SYSTEMIC GRAVITY

The Diagnostic Lens: Three Questions

WHO ARE WE CENTERING?

The “Default” Bias

Platform algorithms center high-engagement users — young, digitally fluent, interaction-rich. Older adults are not deliberately excluded; they simply don’t exist in the default user model. YouTube’s age-protection investments are entirely focused on minors. For users over 65: zero dedicated safeguards, zero simplified interfaces, zero content quality filters.

WHO ARE WE FILTERING OUT?

The Access Barrier

The most vulnerable: 75+, living alone, low-income, non-English speaking. Only 7% of people over 65 even know what a deepfake is. Family can’t help much either — Fiona, a UX designer working in tech, has no idea what her parents watch online. And media literacy programs? The people who need them most are the ones least likely to find them.

WHAT ARE WE EXPLOITING?

The Value System

Their trust and their time. Older adults believe a person in a white coat is a real doctor. They watch for hours. They share with friends. What do platforms give back? Unverified content and echo chambers that reinforce what they already believe. The “Senior Secrets” channel has 300K subscribers and 17M views. Of its 65 cited medical sources, only 5 actually exist. That’s not a glitch. That’s the business model.

Interventions

Three Inclusive Design Interventions

INTERVENTION 1
Rules + Information Flows

Platform Content Equity

What if verified health information was as visible as paid advertising? YouTube marks trusted doctors with a blue panel. Fake doctor channels? Nothing — no warning, no flag. Give certified medical content the same algorithmic weight as paid ads. Stop making misinformation the default.

INTERVENTION 2
Self-Organization

Media Literacy Infrastructure

What if every older adult could take a 1-hour course that moves fake news detection from 64% to 85%? MediaWise, Stanford ($0.22/person), and Candoo Tech already do this. The missing piece is reach — libraries, community centers, younger volunteers pairing up with older adults.

INTERVENTION 3
Feedback Loops

Family Transparency

What if families could share just enough to show care — without it feeling like monitoring? Fiona can’t see what her parents watch. Her parents don’t know what worries her. An opt-in system that shows content categories, not specific videos. Both sides see each other’s patterns. Nobody feels watched.

The Inclusive Design
Intervention #1
April 9, 2026
INTERVENTION 1

Platform Content Equity

Right now, platforms treat all content equally in the algorithm. A video from a board-certified cardiologist competes for attention with an AI-generated fake doctor selling supplements. The algorithm doesn’t care which is real.

Content equity means giving certified medical sources — hospitals, licensed practitioners, public health agencies — the same algorithmic weight as paid advertising. Not suppressing bad content. Elevating good content so it actually competes.

Platforms already do this for minors. YouTube’s entire age-protection system proves they can intervene when they choose to. For older adults, they’ve chosen not to.

THE CURRENT GAP

What platforms do for minors

Age-restricted content, ML-based age estimation, teen safety features, parental controls, content quality filters.

What platforms do for older adults

Nothing. Zero dedicated safeguards. Zero simplified interfaces. Zero health content filters.

The platform paradox

AI slop lowers content costs, raises ad revenue. Platforms profit from the problem. See Case Study: YouTube.

The Inclusive Design
Intervention #2
April 9, 2026
INTERVENTION 2

Media Literacy

Media literacy is the ability to evaluate whether what you’re seeing online is real, sponsored, or fabricated. For older adults facing AI-generated health content, this is the skill gap that matters most.

Platform rules can change. Algorithms can be adjusted. But if people can’t tell a real doctor from a fake one, none of that matters. Teaching people to check sources is the intervention that works regardless of what platforms do.

Distribution channels: public libraries (Be MediaWise Toolkit) and intergenerational pairing (Cyber-Seniors) — reaching older adults in places they already trust.

THREE EXISTING PROGRAMS

MediaWise for Seniors

Poynter Institute

1-hour module. Detection accuracy 64% → 85%. Reached 680,000+ Americans over 50.

Stanford Social Media Lab

Video interventions at $0.22/person. Phase 2 (AI-specific literacy) in development.

Candoo Tech

Focused on health literacy. 60% of users started using telehealth after training.

INTERVENTION 3

Family Transparency

Our ecosystem map identified a broken feedback loop: families can’t verify what older adults see online. Fiona, a UX designer, has no idea what health content her parents consume. Her parents don’t know what concerns her.

Unintended Consequences
April 9, 2026
PLATFORM CONTENT EQUITY

What could go wrong?

Creates a bigger issue?

Certification monopoly

Only well-funded hospitals and pharmaceutical companies can afford certification. Community clinics and patient advocacy groups get pushed down.

Moves pain elsewhere?

Marginalized voices suppressed

The intervention helps older adults but silences smaller, culturally specific health voices that serve the same community.

Incentivizes wrong behavior?

Trust laundering

Bad actors partner with certified entities to legitimize their content. Certification becomes a tool for washing credibility.

REVISED: Tiered certification with multiple pathways + regular audits.

The Inclusive Design
Unintended Consequences
April 9, 2026
MEDIA LITERACY

What could go wrong?

Creates a bigger issue?

Platform’s excuse

If media literacy programs exist, platforms point to them and say “we provided education, it’s the user’s problem now.” Blame shifts from systems to individuals.

Moves pain elsewhere?

Most vulnerable excluded

People who attend workshops are already somewhat connected. The isolated, homebound, non-English-speaking older adults — the ones who need it most — never show up.

Incentivizes wrong behavior?

Overconfidence effect

One hour of training makes people think they can spot anything. AI evolves faster than any curriculum. False confidence is worse than no confidence.

REVISED: Multi-channel delivery + “Education does not replace regulation.”

The Inclusive Design
Unintended Consequences
April 9, 2026
FAMILY TRANSPARENCY

What could go wrong?

Creates a bigger issue?

Self-censorship

Parents know their children can see “health” trending. So they stop searching for sensitive topics — mental health, addiction, STDs. The tool meant to open information flow actually closes it.

Moves pain elsewhere?

Asymmetric power

Designed as bidirectional, but in practice it’s tech-savvy children watching less-capable parents. “Mutual transparency” in name, one-way monitoring in reality.

Incentivizes wrong behavior?

Category gaming

Platforms reclassify health supplement content as “lifestyle” or “wellness” to avoid triggering the sharing alert. The water finds another crack.

REVISED: Weekly digest, not real-time. Sensitive categories can be hidden.

CASE STUDY 2

MediaWise for Seniors

Run by the Poynter Institute, MediaWise for Seniors is a free, 1-hour self-paced course that teaches older adults how to fact-check what they see online. The core skill: lateral reading — instead of judging an article by how it looks, open a new tab and check who wrote it.

Stanford’s Social Media Lab independently verified the results. After taking the course, participants’ ability to identify fake health news jumped from 64% to 85%. The cost per person for video-based interventions: $0.22.

This is what makes media literacy a realistic intervention — not just an ideal. The programs exist, the data is there, and the per-person cost is almost nothing. The bottleneck is reach.
